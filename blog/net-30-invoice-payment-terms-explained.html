Net 30 explained: put a clear due date on your invoice

Understand the starting date, count calendar days and make payment instructions easier for your customer to follow.

By Wolfpack Labs · October 5, 2026 · 3 min read
In this guideAgree on when the clock startsCount calendar days consistentlyMake the payment instructions usableKeep a follow-up planTreat late fees as a separate decision

Agree on when the clock starts

Net 30 commonly describes payment due within 30 days of the invoice date. Intuit lists that convention in its overview of invoice payment terms. Your agreement may instead use receipt of the invoice, delivery or another event.

Confirm the starting point before sending the invoice. An invoice dated earlier than the customer received it can create a dispute when the terms were meant to run from receipt.

Write the terms and the actual due date together. A clear date gives the customer something specific to put into their payment process. Check that it matches the agreement rather than changing the terms at invoicing time.

Count calendar days consistently

When the agreed terms are calendar days from the invoice date, treat the invoice date as day zero and add the term. October 5, 2026 plus 30 calendar days is November 4, 2026.

Terms from October 5, 2026Calendar due date
Net 7October 12, 2026
Net 15October 20, 2026
Net 30November 4, 2026
Net 45November 19, 2026
Net 60December 4, 2026

The Invoice Due Date Calculator uses this calendar-day convention. It also offers a custom number of days and an optional move from a weekend to Monday. That option does not account for holidays and should only be used when it fits your terms.

Make the payment instructions usable

Put the balance due, due date, invoice number and payment method where the customer can find them. If the payment needs a reference, say exactly which one to use.

Example invoice wording

Payment terms: Net 30 from the invoice date.
Invoice date: October 5, 2026.
Payment due: November 4, 2026.
Please include invoice 014 in your payment reference.

If the customer is a business, confirm where to send the invoice and whether a purchase order or supplier reference is needed. A clear due date will not help if the document is sitting in the wrong inbox.

Keep a follow-up plan

Save the invoice you sent and note when to check its status. Before sending a reminder, verify the balance and whether a payment is pending or has already arrived.

The Payment Reminder Pack includes three messages you can adapt. Start with the invoice number, balance and due date, then invite the customer to confirm payment or raise a question.

Record the response and any new arrangement you agree to. Keep the original due date in the history so you can distinguish the original terms from a later conversation.

Treat late fees as a separate decision

A date calculator determines a date. It does not decide whether a late fee is allowed or what rate you should charge. Check your existing agreement and applicable requirements before applying one.

If you already have an appropriate rate and method, the Late Fees Calculator can show the arithmetic. Monthly, annual, daily and one-time rates are different inputs, so match the method to the terms instead of putting the same percentage into every option.

The useful first step is a clear invoice and an accurate payment record. A customer should be able to understand what is owed, when it is due and how to pay without asking you to translate the document.
