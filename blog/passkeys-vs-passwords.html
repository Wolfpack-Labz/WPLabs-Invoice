Passkeys vs. passwords: what’s the difference?

A passkey can let you sign in using the way you already unlock a device. Understanding where it is saved—and how you would recover access—makes that convenience easier to trust.

By Wolfpack Labs · Published October 21, 2026 · 5 min read

Quick answer. A password is a secret you type or autofill. A passkey is a digital credential used by your device or credential manager to prove it can sign in to a particular account. You approve the sign-in with a device unlock, such as a fingerprint, face check, or PIN.

Understand what changes at sign-in

The FIDO Alliance’s passkey explanation describes credentials built around cryptographic keys rather than a reusable password shared with a website. The website can verify a sign-in without receiving your private key.

Everyday differences between the two sign-in methods
QuestionPasswordPasskey
What do I do?Type or autofill a password.Approve a sign-in through a supported device or credential manager.
What do I remember?The password, or how to unlock its manager.How to unlock the device or manager holding the credential.
Can a fake sign-in page collect it like a password?A typed password can be captured.Passkeys are bound to the intended service and resist that form of phishing.

“Passkey” is the name of the sign-in technology, not a particular brand of phone. Support and the prompts you see depend on the account, device, browser, and credential provider.

Separate your face or PIN from the account credential

A face scan is not itself the passkey. It is one way your device can authorize use of the saved credential. FIDO explains that biometric processing stays local; the remote service receives confirmation of a successful check rather than your fingerprint or face data.

For a personal Google Account, Google’s passkey instructions allow sign-in with a fingerprint, face scan, or screen lock. They also explain that adding a passkey does not remove existing authentication or recovery factors.

That does not mean every service handles older sign-in methods the same way. Read the account’s own security settings before assuming its password has disappeared or its recovery process has changed.

Check where your passkey will be saved

Some passkeys can sync through a credential provider; others stay bound to a device or hardware security key. Do not assume that every passkey automatically appears on every phone or computer you own.

During setup, pause at the storage prompt. Note the provider or device it names and the account you are using with that provider. This is more useful than trying to remember the exact appearance of the sign-in button.

A short personal note could say: “Shopping account: passkey saved in [Provider], under my [Personal account]. Recovery checked on [Date].” Keep passwords and recovery codes out of an ordinary notes file. The note is a map to your setup, not a copy of its secrets.

Try one account on a device you control

  1. Open the service through its official app or a known website address.
  2. Review its security settings and current recovery options.
  3. Find the passkey option and read which provider or device will save it.
  4. Follow the device-unlock prompt and confirm the new credential appears in the account settings.
  5. Use the provider’s instructions to check a normal sign-in before removing any other access method.

For iPhone-specific steps, use Apple’s guide to signing in with passkeys. Apple notes that the flow varies by app, site, or browser, so a button’s wording may differ from another person’s screen.

Google specifically warns against creating a personal passkey on a shared device: someone who can unlock that device may be able to use it. For a work or school account, check your organization’s policy; an administrator can control the available sign-in options.

Plan for a replacement or missing device

Before depending on a new sign-in method, work through a practical scenario: your usual phone is unavailable, and you need to reach the account from another device. Which provider holds the credential? How would you reach that provider? What account-recovery options are available?

  • Check that recovery contact information is current.
  • Follow the service’s instructions for any recovery codes or additional credentials it offers.
  • Understand whether your setup syncs credentials or needs a separate device.
  • Before selling a phone, follow the device and account providers’ removal instructions.

These are questions to answer for your setup, not a promise that a single recovery method works everywhere. Avoid removing the last working access method while you are still learning how the replacement works.

Keep checking the requests that reach you

Passkeys help protect authentication. They do not make every email, payment request, or attachment genuine. A scammer can still try to persuade you to send money or disclose information without stealing your passkey.

The FTC’s phishing guidance recommends checking suspicious requests through a number or website you already know is real. Use that habit alongside stronger sign-in.
